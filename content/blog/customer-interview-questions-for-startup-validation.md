You are about to talk to potential users for the first time, and you are afraid your questions will lead them. You are right to worry. People are polite, and they will tell you your idea sounds great.

By the end of this post you will have a seven-question script, six bad questions rewritten as good ones, a way to score each call for real evidence, a plan for finding your first ten people, and a note template. You can run your first call this week.

## The one idea: ask about their life, not your idea

The best-known guide here is Rob Fitzpatrick's [The Mom Test](https://www.momtestbook.com/). Its premise is that even your mom can give you useful information if you stop asking whether your idea is good. Ask about their life instead.

A [summary of the book](https://www.shortform.com/blog/the-mom-test-book/) boils it down to three rules: don't pitch your idea, ask specific questions about the past, and listen more than you talk. A [second summary](https://www.supersummary.com/the-mom-test/summary/) lists the three kinds of bad data Fitzpatrick warns about: compliments, fluff and ideas.

The logic holds up without the book. A question about the future ("Would you use this?") asks the person to predict their own behavior, and they are bad at that. They also want to be nice to you. A question about the past ("When did you last deal with this?") asks for a fact. Facts are hard to inflate.

A rule of thumb that follows from this: if your question would still make sense after you swap in a competitor's product, or a different idea entirely, it is probably about your idea and not about their life. Rewrite it.

## Six bad questions, rewritten

Each bad question below invites an opinion or a compliment. Each good version asks for a specific past event.

| Bad question | Why it fails | Better version |
|---|---|---|
| "Do you think this is a good idea?" | Invites a compliment. | "How do you handle [the task] today?" |
| "Would you pay for a tool that does X?" | Predicts the future. Saying yes is free. | "What do you currently pay for, in money or hours, to deal with X?" |
| "Is X a big problem for you?" | Leading, and "big" means nothing. | "Tell me about the last time X happened. What did you do?" |
| "Would you use an app that tracks Y?" | Hypothetical. | "Do you track Y now? Show me how." |
| "How often do you think you'd use it?" | A guess about a product that does not exist. | "How many times did this come up last month?" |
| "What features would you want?" | Gets you ideas, not evidence. You are the designer. | "What have you already tried? Why did you stop?" |

Notice the pattern. The good versions use "last time," "currently," "already" and "show me." Those words pull the person into a real memory.

## A short script you can run in 20 minutes

You do not need 50 questions. You need about seven, plus follow-ups. Copy this.

1. **Context.** "Tell me about your role and what a normal week looks like."
2. **Last time.** "Walk me through the last time [problem] came up. What happened first?"
3. **Cost.** "How long did that take? What did it cost you, in time, money or a missed deadline?"
4. **Current fix.** "What did you do about it? Did you try anything else?"
5. **Money.** "Are you paying for anything related to this now? A tool, a freelancer, a spreadsheet template? How much?"
6. **Priority.** "Where does this rank among your frustrations right now?"
7. **Referral.** "Who else deals with this? Could you introduce me?"

Then use follow-ups that keep you in the past: "Why was that hard?" "What happened next?" "Can you show me?" When someone says "I always" or "I would," ask for the most recent actual time.

Do not pitch in the first 15 minutes. If you have an idea, hold it back. When they ask what you are building, give one plain sentence and go back to their situation. If you want to test a pitch, do it at the end, and treat what comes after it (a commitment, not a reaction) as the data.

## Signals that count versus compliments

After the call, sort what you heard into two piles. The first pile is cheap talk. The second is costly behavior.

**Compliments and noise (count as zero):**

- "That's a really cool idea."
- "I'd definitely use that."
- "You should build it."
- Feature wishes with no story behind them.

**Evidence (count it, and write it down):**

- **A workaround.** They built a spreadsheet, a script, a Zapier chain or a manual weekly routine. Effort already spent is a strong sign.
- **Money spent.** They pay for a competing tool, a contractor or a template. Ask for the number.
- **Time spent.** They can say it eats three hours every Friday, and they can describe those hours.
- **They asked to be notified.** They want to hear when you ship, without you asking for an email.
- **They offered a cost.** An intro to their boss, a follow-up call, access to their data, a deposit or a pre-order.

The Mom Test summaries describe the later stage in terms of commitment and advancement: the person gives up something (time, reputation, money) or moves the deal forward. That is the right frame for deciding what to count. A compliment costs them nothing. A spent hour or a dollar does.

This method can mislead you in two ways. A person can have a real problem and a workaround and still not pay for a fix, because the pain is real but small. And a loud, articulate person can dominate your impression. That is why you count across several interviews, not one.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

Say a developer, Priya, is thinking about a tool that auto-reconciles Stripe payouts for small agencies. She talks to ten agency owners.

In her first two calls she asks, "Would you use something that reconciles payouts automatically?" Both say yes. She learns nothing she can act on.

She rewrites the script around "walk me through the last time you reconciled payouts." Here are her results across the next eight calls:

| Evidence | Count (of 8) |
|---|---|
| Described a specific recent incident | 7 |
| Has a workaround (spreadsheet or bookkeeper) | 5 |
| Pays someone for it today | 2 (one $150/month bookkeeper add-on, one 4 hours of a freelancer's time per month) |
| Spends over 2 hours per month on it | 3 |
| Asked to be notified or offered an intro | 2 |

Her reading: the problem exists, but only 2 of 8 pay and 3 of 8 lose real time. That is a narrow segment, not a mass market. The next step is not building. She narrows to agencies with 10 or more clients, where the time cost was highest, and does five more calls. If the paying share rises, she tests a manual service or a pre-sale. If it stays at 2 of 8, she drops it and has lost two weeks, not three months.

The thresholds above are illustrative, not a published benchmark. Set your own before you start, so you cannot talk yourself into a pass afterward. A reasonable starting rule: at least half of a tight segment has a workaround, and at least a quarter already spend money or serious time.

## How to find your first ten people

Aim for ten people who match one narrow description. "Developers" is too wide. "Freelance developers who invoice 5 or more clients" is findable.

Good places to look, roughly in order of response rate:

1. **People you already know in the segment.** Not friends as a favor, but colleagues and former coworkers who actually have the problem. Ask them for intros too.
2. **Communities where the problem is already discussed.** Search a subreddit, Slack or forum for complaints about the task. Message the person who posted, refer to their post, and ask for 15 minutes about it. Do not pitch.
3. **Reviews and complaints about existing tools.** Reviewers who describe a workaround are pre-qualified.
4. **Referrals from every call.** The last question in the script exists for this reason. Ten calls can come from three.
5. **Cold email or LinkedIn.** Lower response, but you can target exactly. Keep it to three sentences.

A cold message you can copy:

> Hi [name], I'm a developer researching how [role] handle [task]. I'm not selling anything. Could I ask you 15 minutes of questions about the last time you dealt with it? Happy to share what I learn.

Ask for a short, specific slot. Offer to work around their schedule. The advice to treat customer discovery as getting out of the building is Steve Blank's, and his [customer discovery archive](https://steveblank.com/tag/customer-discovery/) is a good read if you want the wider method behind it. If you have not yet picked the idea to test, see [how to validate a startup idea before building an MVP](/blog/how-to-validate-a-startup-idea-before-building-mvp).

One note: friends and family are the worst pool, which is the whole point of the book's title. If you must start with them, ask only about their past behavior, and treat any enthusiasm as noise.

## A note template for each interview

Write notes within an hour of the call, while you remember it. Separate what they said from what you think. Copy this into a doc and fill one per call.

```
Name / role / company size:
Date / source (how I found them):
Segment fit (1-5):

Last time the problem came up (what happened):
Time cost (their number, in their words):
Money cost / what they pay now:
Workaround or tools used:
What they tried and abandoned:

EVIDENCE (costly behavior)
[ ] Workaround   [ ] Pays today   [ ] Spends hours
[ ] Asked to be notified   [ ] Offered intro / data / deposit

NOISE (compliments, hypotheticals, feature wishes):

Surprises (things I did not expect):
Quotes worth keeping (exact words):
Next step they agreed to (specific, dated):
Referrals given:

My mistakes (did I pitch? lead? talk too much?):
```

The last field matters. Most first-time interviewers pitch in minute five. Naming that mistake in writing makes the next call better.

After ten calls, put the evidence checkboxes in one table, like Priya's. Patterns show up fast. If the same workaround appears in four calls, that is your product's real competitor.

## What to do this week

1. **Day 1.** Write one sentence naming your segment and the problem. Set your pass rule, such as "at least 4 of 10 have a workaround and 2 of 10 pay."
2. **Day 1.** Copy the seven-question script and the note template into a doc. Cross out any question that mentions your idea.
3. **Day 2.** List 15 people you could reach: five you know, five from communities, five from tool reviews. Send the message above to all 15.
4. **Days 3 to 5.** Run at least three calls. Record them if the person agrees, or take notes live and fill in the template right after.
5. **After each call.** Ask for two introductions. Count the evidence checkboxes.
6. **End of week.** Review your first three notes for pitching and leading. Fix the script, then book the next seven.

Do not start building until you have ten calls and a pass rule you set beforehand. If the evidence is weak, that is a cheap result. You found out in a week.
